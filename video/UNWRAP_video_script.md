# UNWRAP – 5-minute video script (CM617)

Target: ~5:00 at a relaxed ~135 words per minute (about 585 spoken words; the video is timed for ~4:50 at a calm ~122 words per minute).
Each scene has the voice-over (VO, also used as the subtitle text) and what appears on screen, timed to the words.
**Bold** words in the VO are where a new visual pops in.

---

## Scene 0 – Title card (0:00 – 0:04, no voice-over)
**On screen:** centred title “UNWRAP – Unpacking Whether Replacement Alternatives Perform”, then the team names (Paridhi Mittal · Eshika Mishra · Keshav Goyal · Kunal Jain) and “CM617 Sustainability Principles and Practices · IIT Bombay”, while five pack icons drop in below.

---

## Scene 1 – Hook (0:00 – 0:25)
**VO:** Picture a plastic bottle and a glass bottle. Which one is better for the planet? Almost everyone says **glass**. We checked **thirty-one** real comparisons from published studies. Replacements for plastic had a lower carbon footprint in only **thirteen** of them. So, is "plastic-free" really greener?

**On screen:** plastic bottle vs glass bottle appear, a green tick hovers over glass. Counter runs up to 31, then 13 lights up and the tick cracks / turns into a "?".

## Scene 2 – The problem (0:25 – 0:58)
**VO:** Governments and brands are pushing to replace plastic with **glass, aluminium, cartons** and **refills**. The carbon case comes from life cycle assessments. But these studies have three problems. One: they **disagree** with each other. Two: they assume **European** waste systems, where most material is recycled, while in India a lot of waste is never collected. Three: they measure the pack per kilogram of product **filled**, not per kilogram actually **used**.

**On screen:** four pack icons drop in with each material name. Three numbered cards slide in (disagree = arrows pointing opposite ways; Europe map vs India map; a half-empty shampoo bottle with product stuck inside).

## Scene 3 – Our approach (0:58 – 1:25)
**VO:** So we asked three questions. **One**: is replacement packaging actually lower in carbon than the plastic it replaces? **Two**: does that change under Indian collection rates? **Three**: does the answer change if we count the product left stuck in the pack? We built **three stages** to answer them: a meta-analysis, a recycling model, and an honest functional unit.

**On screen:** H1, H2, H3 labels appear one at a time and each connects to Stage 1, Stage 2, Stage 3 boxes on a three-step path.

## Scene 4 – Stage 1: paired meta-analysis (1:25 – 2:25)
**VO:** In stage one we screened **ninety** records and kept **twenty-four** reports. Instead of comparing averages across different studies, we only compared packs **inside the same study**, where both used the same assumptions. That gave us **thirty-one pairs** from sixteen studies. The result: on average, the alternative had **1.38 times** the footprint of plastic. But it depends on the material. **Cartons** beat plastic in **all nine** pairs, at 0.63 times. **Reusable glass** is close to a tie. **Single-use glass** is 2.6 times worse and **aluminium** is 2.5 times worse.
Then a twist. When a study was **paid for by a material maker**, that material came out best in **four of five** studies. And values copied from an **industry-compiled table** looked **26 percent** kinder to alternatives than the original papers.

**On screen:** funnel 90 → 52 → 24 → 31 pairs. Forest-plot dots fall into place per material; the bars for carton (green), reusable glass (grey), glass and aluminium (red) grow with the numbers. A coin / money-bag icon appears next to "sponsor" with "4 of 5" and a magnifier over "industry table: 26%".

## Scene 5 – Stage 2: India's collection rates (2:25 – 3:20)
**VO:** Stage two moves to India. We built a simulation of a beverage can, a PET bottle and a glass bottle, using **Indian recycling rates**: eighty-five percent for aluminium, seventy for PET. In Europe, an aluminium can has **1.73 times** the footprint of PET. In India, that penalty shrinks to **1.16**. Better, but still not a win. Aluminium only matches PET if about **eighty-nine percent** of cans are collected, and India is at **eighty-five**. So close, and yet not there. Glass stays about **five times** worse than PET at any collection rate.

**On screen:** a slider labelled "collection rate" moves from Europe to India; the aluminium bar shortens from 1.73 to 1.16. A finish-line at 89% with a runner stopping at 85%. Glass bar stays tall.

## Scene 6 – Stage 3: the honest functional unit (3:20 – 4:20)
**VO:** Stage three is our favourite. Every packaging study we found counts a pack per kilogram of shampoo **filled**. But product that stays stuck inside the pack is **wasted**, and its carbon is wasted too. Using measured residue data from published studies, we scored four shampoo packs: a **sachet**, a **pump bottle**, a **refill pouch** and a **two-litre canister**. When we charged each pack for the product it strands, every footprint went **up**, by **thirty-eight to one hundred twenty-one percent**. The big canister, which **tied** with the refill pouch on the usual measure, **lost** by **1.4 times** once leftovers were counted. And the sachet, usually the villain, turned out to be only **partly** worse than a pump bottle. Refill pouches won either way.

**On screen:** four shampoo packs line up. Leftover shampoo drips and pools, filling a "wasted" meter on each pack. Two bars per pack (grey "per kg filled", purple "per kg used") — the purple ones stretch up. Canister vs pouch ranking swaps places with an animation.

## Scene 7 – Conclusion (4:20 – 5:00)
**VO:** So what did we learn? Plastic replacement is **not automatically greener**. Cartons reliably win, reuse helps, and single-use glass and aluminium usually lose. In India, **collection rates** change the answer, especially for aluminium. And packaging should be judged **per unit of product actually used**, under **local waste systems**, with **independent** evidence. Next time someone says "plastic-free", ask: compared to what, where, and who paid for the study? Thank you.

**On screen:** three take-away cards stack up (Material · Place · Unit of use). Final line "Compared to what? Where? Who paid?" types out, then team names and the IIT Bombay logo text.
